Every playthrough brings different patients and problems.

  • Pregunta: what’s wrong, where it hurts, since when, what happened. Adults: usted (¿Qué le pasa? ¿Le duele…?). Teens: tú (¿Qué te pasa? ¿Te duele…?).
  • Aconseja: give at least two pieces of advice with tener que (Tiene que descansar. Tienes que beber agua.) and recommend something from the shelf (click it).
  • One patient needs a doctor, not the pharmacy. Spot them!
  • La charla: tell a group of kids the beach rules with ustedes commands (Pónganse bloqueador.).

      Para el profe: how this scene works
      • Los pacientes: four per playthrough (two adults, two teens), drawn from seven everyday problems (sunburn, sprained ankle, cold, coral cut, mosquito bites, upset stomach, too much sun) plus one case that needs a doctor (high fever for three days, a badly swollen ankle after a fall, or a deep cut that won’t stop bleeding). Patients only say what they’re asked: what’s wrong (¿Qué le pasa?), where it hurts (¿Dónde le duele? ¿Le duele la cabeza?), since when (¿Desde cuándo?, answered with hace dos días, modelled), what happened (answered in the preterite, modelled), fever and other symptoms. Answers fill in the patient file.
      • Advice: at least two good pieces of advice, with tener que + infinitive (Tiene que ponerse crema de aloe. Tienes que descansar.); for teens, affirmative tú commands also count (¡Bebe agua!). Plus one suitable product from the shelf (click to insert Le/Te recomiendo…). Bad advice (tomar sol with a sunburn, correr with a sprain, rascarse with bites) is caught, with negation understood (no tomar sol is good advice). For the serious case, the right answer is Tiene que ir al médico / al EBAIS; selling a product instead gets corrected by Doña Fefi.
      • Checked forms: register (usted with adults, tú with teens) in questions and advice; tener que + infinitive (tienes que bebe → tienes que beber; tiene que ponerte → ponerse); doler agreement (¿Le duelen la cabeza? → ¿Le duele…?, ¿Te duele los pies? → ¿Te duelen…?).
      • La charla de seguridad: the lifeguard asks for the beach rules for a group of kids, from a poster of pictures (sun cream, water, cap, red flag, lifeguard, rip current, never alone). At least four affirmative ustedes commands, three of them about the poster (Pónganse bloqueador. Beban agua. Naden cerca del salvavidas. Si hay corriente, naden paralelo a la playa.). Tú commands to a group (ponte → pónganse), statements (usan → usen), infinitives (usar → usen) and usted forms (use → usen) are corrected; written accents on pónganse, quédense are noted. The rip-current advice (swim parallel to the shore) is real Costa Rican beach safety.
      • Hidden gems: six Datos de Costa Rica: ¡pura vida!, the sloth, the howler monkey (mono congo), sea turtles, the Caribbean patí (with Limón’s Afro-Caribbean heritage) and the EBAIS clinic. Costa Rican usage: zancudos (mosquitoes), mae, diay, tuanis, upe, ¡qué chiva!, and Doña Fefi’s note on ustedeo (many Costa Ricans use usted even with friends and family).
      • Outcomes practised (20-3Y): LC–1.3 a (health and safety: physical states, a visit to the doctor) · LC–1.4 b (tener que + infinitive; affirmative tú and ustedes commands; reflexive verbs) · LC–1.4 a (preterite, hace + time, modelled) · LC–1.4 c (ser, estar, tener) · A–3.1 a, d, e (requests, encouraging or discouraging a course of action, instructions) · A–1.1 a · A–2.2 a (inquire about and respond to feelings) · A–4.3 a–b (recognize a problem and solve it) · LC–2.1 a · LC–3.1 a · LC–3.2 a · LC–3.3 a (the poster) · LC–4.1 a–b · LC–4.4 a · GC–1.2 b · GC–1.4 a · GC–2.2 a.
      • This is a language game, not first-aid training: the advice is simple and general, and the serious cases always go to a doctor.
