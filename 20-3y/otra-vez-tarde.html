Every playthrough is a different morning: new times, a new lost item, new weather.

  • Give Mateo commands in Spanish: ¡Levántate! ¡Dúchate! ¡Desayuna! You can also chain them: Levántate y dúchate.
  • Watch the clock. Read the family board, and listen to the radio and to messages: they tell you who is in the bathroom, the weather and more.
  • If Mateo misses la ruta or forgets something, it’s Monday again. Use what you learned.

          Para el profe: how this scene works
          • La mañana is a time-loop puzzle. The student is an exchange student staying with Mateo’s family and gives him affirmative tú commands: ¡Levántate! ¡Báñate! ¡Lávate los dientes! ¡Vístete! ¡Desayuna! ¡Alista la mochila! ¡Lleva la sombrilla! ¡Deja el celular! ¡Sal! Tienes que + infinitive also works (Tienes que bañarte). Each command costs minutes. Mateo has to reach the bus stop before la ruta with everything done. If he doesn’t, the summary of the day is told in the preterite and imperfect (modelled, not marked), and Monday starts again. What the student discovered goes onto the family board as notes.
          • Randomized each playthrough: alarm and bus times, who uses the bathroom and when (Papá se afeita, Valentina se ducha), the lost item (notebook, calculator or school ID) and where it is, the weather on the radio (rain → sombrilla/paraguas; cold → chaqueta/ruana), whether the driver texts that he’s coming early, and when Mateo gets stuck on his phone. A solver checks that every morning can be done with a few minutes to spare.
          • Feedback on form, never a fail: a bare verb (Levanta) gets a joke and a reminder that it’s reflexive (¿Que levante qué?); usted forms (Levántese) get a register note: Mateo is a teenager, so tú; statements (Te levantas), infinitives and first-person forms get a nudge towards the command. Missing written accents (levantate → levántate) and the stem change (vestete → vístete) are pointed out gently while the command still works.
          • Tu rutina: Mateo asks about the student’s routine. The checker looks for at least three first-person reflexive verbs (me levanto, me baño, me visto…), two sequence words (primero, luego, después, por último…) and a time. It corrects yo levanto → me levanto, me levanta → me levanto, me vesto → me visto. Mateo then compares their morning with his (Bogotá students often start school around 6:30–7:00).
          • Hidden gems: six Datos de Bogotá to click: Monserrate, la ruana, chocolate santafereño with cheese, el tinto, pico y placa and la ruta. Colombian usage appears in the dialogue: bañarse for showering, sombrilla, alistar, trancón, ¡Qué pena!, un momentico, madrugar.
          • Outcomes practised (20-3Y): A–3.1 a, d, e (requests, discouraging an action, a sequence of instructions) · A–4.3 a–b (recognize a problem and solve it) · A–1.1 b (sequences of actions) · LC–1.3 a (daily routine) · LC–1.4 b (reflexive verbs, affirmative tú commands, tener que + infinitive, present progressive) · LC–1.4 a (preterite and imperfect, modelled) · LC–2.1 a (radio and messages) · LC–3.1 a · LC–3.2 a · LC–4.1 a–b (tú vs. usted) · LC–5.1 a (cohesion markers) · GC–1.2 a (things in common with peers in Bogotá) · GC–2.2 a (different words for the same thing).
